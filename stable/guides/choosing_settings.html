Choosing Settings#

Four settings decide most of a study’s results: which meteorology, how long the particles run (n_hours), how many there are (numpar), and the footprint grid. Each has a default that gets a project started, but the right value depends on your site and your question. Here is how we choose each one, and a quick check that tells you whether the choice was good enough.

Meteorology#

Use the finest product that covers your place and years, because the valleys, coastlines and mixed-layer depth that steer air near a city are smoothed away on a coarse grid. In the contiguous US since mid-2015 that is HRRR at 3 km. Meteorology has a table of the products and when to use each. To see how much the choice matters for your site, run the same receptors with a second met as a second variant and compare the footprints.

How long to run#

n_hours should be long enough for the air to leave the region whose fluxes you care about, because a flux the particles never reached is missing from the modeled enhancement. We start with 24 hours for a tower in a city and 72 hours or more for a regional network or a remote site. The check is the particles themselves: how long they ran, and how many are still inside the footprint grid at the end.

In [1]: import stilt

In [2]: project = stilt.Project("./my_project")

In [3]: sim = project.simulation("202101150800_-111.848_40.766_10", "hrrr")

In [4]: ends = sim.particles.stilt.endpoints()

In [5]: print(f"the longest particle ran {ends['age'].abs().max() / 60:.1f} of 24 hours")
the longest particle ran 12.0 of 24 hours

These particles stopped after 13.5 of the 24 hours, because they left the small crop of met files these pages run on. The met’s domain, not n_hours, limited this footprint, so a study would widen the crop (Meteorology). When the particles do run the whole time and many of them end inside the grid, a longer run would add footprint, so raise n_hours.

How many particles#

More particles give a smoother footprint, and the far part of a footprint, where few particles reach, needs the most. The default of 200 shows the shape of a footprint. For results we use 1000, STILT-R’s default. To check a value for your site, run a few receptors with three times as many particles as a second variant and compare:

variants:
  hrrr: {}
  hrrr-np3k: {numpar: 3000}

If the enhancements you model from the two differ by much less than the other errors in your study, such as the transport error (Transport Error), the lower count is enough.

The footprint grid#

The grid must cover where the air comes from over the whole run, because particles outside it add nothing to the footprint. Plot the particles of a few receptors from different seasons and times of day, and draw the grid around all of them.

Make the cells as fine as the finest flux inventory you will multiply the footprints by, so that every inventory is as coarse as the footprint or coarser (Summing Footprints: Areas And The Jacobian, “Footprints times a flux”). We use 0.01° (about 1 km) for a city and 0.1° for a region.

What else to set#

Most projects leave the other settings at their defaults, which follow STILT-R. Three are worth knowing:

  • krand: 2 with a seed makes the particles the same on every run, so a rerun or a colleague’s run gives the same footprint (Your First Footprint).

  • hnf_plume, the near-field correction, is on by default and matters for a receptor near strong sources (Checking A Run).

  • A transport-error variant estimates how much the wind errors of the meteorology change the modeled enhancement (Transport Error).
